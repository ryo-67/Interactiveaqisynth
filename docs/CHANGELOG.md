## 2026-09-16 — Five performance changes with no visible cost

A friend's old MacBook lags. From the audit (the sky at device resolution through thirteen full-screen passes is the likely first cause; the main thread's per-frame work the second), Shoro parked every change that alters the picture or the sound and took the five that do not. One easing loop: seventeen `useEased` hooks each owned an animation-frame loop that set React state, up to ten loops a frame in the profile, each committing the page tree on its own; now every eased value advances in one callback on one timestamp and subscribers are told once, so React commits a frame's values together (2.7 loops a frame at rest, from 8.5). The frost's rectangles are read every frame only while the scene has declared a movement, a switch and its fade, a drag, a resize, the fonts arriving, a glass changing size, and four times a second otherwise as the safety net; at rest that is 64 layout reads a second instead of about a thousand. The graph's fill keeps its alpha mask, a per-pixel loop over the plot, in its own cache keyed on the geometry, so a lift easing after a beat repaints only the colour columns. The canvas has no multisampling, which the composer's own targets never used. The lens shader returns the frame untouched at level zero rather than running its 28-lens loop per pixel to produce nothing. Verified: build, tests, the frost's rectangles within a pixel of every panel at rest and after a switch each way, and the meters still easing while playing. Not measured: the GPU savings, which the headless profiler renders in software; those are the friend's machine to confirm.

## 2026-09-16 — One ramp family, one gradient for line and legend (D-51)

Shoro: the line and the legend disagreed in some views, and the ramp's colours were not one family, green and yellow bright, red, magenta and purple washed out. Two causes. The ends had been hand-picked per hue as the most saturated colour at the luminance 3:1 needed, which put yellow at 0.93 luminance beside a red at 0.28. The ramp is now built in OKLCH (utils/oklch.ts): six hues at one lightness and one chroma per end, the chroma held to what purple, the least able hue, can carry, so the six read as one family; the dark end clears AA (4.6 to 5.1) on the darkest panel and the light end 3.9 to 4.2 on the brightest, Shoro's "AA or close across every variation". Blends along the ramp and between the ends are in OKLCH too, so no midpoint dips toward grey. The second cause was the graph: each hour carried a stored colour, blended between frames in sRGB during a morph, so a line halfway between a green day and a purple one showed grey at a height where the legend showed red, and a segment's two-stop gradient on a steep rise skipped the hues between its ends. The line, the dots, the trailing dashes and the fill's columns now read one vertical ramp gradient over the plot's height, the same stops the legend is drawn from, so colour is a function of height and cannot disagree. The AQI card's bar fills to the value's position along the ramp, the ladders' continuous form, rather than the number over 500 (Shoro). The frost's blur and saturation were raised at the same time: the mipmap blur is sized in CSS pixels so its reach no longer shrinks with the device pixel ratio, and the saturation is applied in sRGB as the CSS filter was (Shoro: less blur and less colour than before). Also fixed: the frame's 40 px reach had put the band's pointer surface over the bars' pills below laptop, so chips answered only along their far edge and the presets not at all on a phone; the track, which is exactly the band, takes the pointer now, and a page's compositing ring around its section takes none. And on a Mac trackpad the sideways swipe back to the AQI page had also fired the browser's back navigation, since a page that cannot scroll sideways hands an unconsumed horizontal gesture to the browser; the root now declares that it owns its overscroll (`overscroll-behavior: none`), which Chromium and Firefox honour and Safari from 16, so the swipe turns the page and nothing else.

## 2026-09-16 — The sky draws the frost (D-50)

The band across the frosted cards came back, this time on Firefox on Shoro's new laptop, on random cursor movement. Shoro's bisect with the `?fx=` switches: it reproduced with every sky layer removed and vanished only with the DOM cursor or the blur removed. The mechanism is Firefox's partial re-blur: WebRender re-renders only the region a moving element damaged, so the cursor's path dirties a strip across a card, the backdrop blur is recomputed for that strip alone, and a blur over a sub-rectangle lands on a different sampling grid from the cached whole. One blur for both materials now (`GLASS.frost`), tuned by eye against the 18 and 28 px it replaced.

## 2026-09-15 — A change of day is a sunset and a sunrise (D-29); golden hour; eased inputs refuse NaN

## 2026-09-15 — Dawn rises once

The sky brightened at 6 am, darkened at 7, then brightened again at 8. Measured through the Clear Day at quarter-hour steps (mean luminance of the sky band): 82 at night, a 125 spike as the night model's horizon glow arrived, then 43 as the day model took over at its noon exposure — a crater no real dawn has. The causes were structural. At equal exposure the day model at 6° elevation is darker than the night sky and far darker than the night model's dawn glow, and the night model keeps brightening faster than the day model as the sun climbs, so any hand-over above the horizon slid downhill; and the night-blue layer faded before anything replaced it. The hand-over now happens across the horizon itself, −2° to 2°, before the night model's glow appears; the night is darker (exposure 0.45, not 0.65) so a dawn can rise from it; the blue fades out at the horizon; and exposure is a measured curve over sun elevation, the camera's auto-exposure, set point by point so the band rises through dawn and on to noon and falls the same way through dusk. Result: 69 at night, 81 at the horizon, 90 → 107 across the first three hours, 142 by late morning; dusk 101 → 95 → 83 → 81. Sunrise and sunset themselves are astronomical for the date; no weather is modelled, since the data sources carry none.
